01 — The Brief

Taco Bell's First Mobile Order App.

Taco Bell came to Digitas wanting the best-in-class mobile ordering app in their category. We won the pitch. The delivery team was a focused trio: an Art Director, a Copywriter, and myself as the UX Designer. A third-party development team would build it.

The objectives were specific. A streamlined ordering flow. Full customization. A return path for repeat customers that felt faster than the line at the counter. Underneath those, a quieter requirement: the app had to feel like Taco Bell. Loud, irreverent, a little ridiculous. The interface couldn't sand any of that down.

This was 2012. There was no playbook for what a fast-food ordering app should be. Most quick-service brands didn't have one yet. We were defining the category as we built it.

How do you make ordering as fast as talking to a cashier?
Can full customization feel effortless, not overwhelming?
What does a return path feel like when the brand is loud and irreverent?
How do you define the category when there is no category yet?
02 — Team & Process

Three on the Project. Five in the Room.

Officially, the project team was three: art direction, copy, and me. Unofficially, we worked inside a group of five designers led by one VP, each running independent projects but sharing a studio, a whiteboard, and a daily rhythm of pulling each other into the work.

That setup is the one I've been trying to recreate ever since. Independent ownership, shared atmosphere. Crits that happened because someone wheeled their chair over. Challenges that came from the next desk over and made the work sharper. Camaraderie was the engine of the daily operation. When I think about what "good team" feels like now, this is the room I'm comparing it to.

"The choreography only worked because the people involved trusted each other to deliver."

The Agile cadence put pressure on that trust. I ran a few sprints ahead of the Art Director — providing interaction design and structure through wireframes, flows, and sitemaps. The Art Director ran ahead of the third-party developers with visual comps. Nobody waited. Nobody was blocked.

Tight feedback loops kept velocity honest. Between sprints, the three of us pulled the rest of the studio in for crits, folded client input into the next round, and kept iteration cycles short by design — so course corrections happened early, when they were cheap.

03 — Designing the Menu

Customization Without the Cognitive Load.

The menu was the heart of the product. A Taco Bell order is rarely a single item. It's a combo, a customization, a swap, an add-on, and a side — often built faster than a cashier can ring it up. The interface had to keep up with that thinking, not slow it down.

The design I landed on split the screen vertically: a condensed list of menu categories on the left that stayed put as the user scrolled, and the item list on the right that scrolled independently. Tap a category, the item list jumped. Scroll past the last item in one category, the list rolled into the next and the category selection updated to match. Swipe right, the item list collapsed and full-width categories returned.

That construct sounds straightforward written down. It took dozens of paper sketches, low-fidelity wireframes, and a lot of on-screen prototyping to find. I obsessed over the details: how the categories should feel when the scroll crossed a boundary, how a tap should differ from a swipe, how the "+" button should behave when no store had been selected yet.

Ad-hoc user research kept the work honest along the way. I'd grab whoever was free in the halls, hand them my phone with the prototype loaded, and watch where they hesitated. Later, lab usability testing validated the construct at higher fidelity. The app scored well. The few changes that came out of formal testing were minor, because the rough edges had already been found in the hallway.

Apply the principles, then prototype until the tactile feel proves them out. Reading about interaction design only gets you so far. You have to put it in your hand.

04 — Rotate to Reorder

The Best Idea From Playing With the Medium.

We could have designed a "Reorder" button. Tap, confirm, done. It would have been fine. The conventional flow was sitting right there, waiting to be implemented.

Instead, the team played. What if the gesture of flipping your phone sideways was the reorder?

That became Rotate to Reorder. Tilt the phone into landscape and the app shifted into a fast-access view of saved orders, named the way the user named them — The 1AM Wonder, The Hot Tamale, Late Night Savior. One tap to add a saved order to the cart. The interaction was playful, fast, and unmistakably Taco Bell.

I'm a named inventor on the patent for the feature.

The conventional flow is rarely the most interesting one. The medium itself — the hardware, the gesture vocabulary, the constraint of a small screen — has more to give than a checklist of features will surface. Playing with the medium is how you find the design that becomes the thing people remember.

05 — Research

Usability Testing at Every Resolution.

Three tiers of testing ran in parallel with the design work — each one calibrated to the fidelity of what was ready to show and what question needed answering.

5.1

Ad-hoc tests around the office

Recruiting whoever was free the moment a wireframe was solid enough to point at. Cheap, fast, embarrassing when something obvious failed — which was exactly the point. These sessions caught rough edges early, when changes were still inexpensive.

5.2

Remote unmoderated tests via verifyapp.com

Once the screens were further along. Wider sample, asynchronous, useful for the questions that needed a number behind them. The distance from moderation surfaced behaviors that in-person presence can sometimes suppress.

5.3

Lab usability testing in the final stages

Run by an outside firm in a real testing facility with one-way glass and a moderator. The app scored well. The few changes that came out of it were minor — because the rough edges had already been found in the hallway.

Test at the resolution you can afford, when you can afford it. Don't wait for the formal study to find the obvious problem. That principle has saved more projects than the formal studies have.
